Changelog

  • 2026-09-24: The About page now shows whether players improve: how fast ranks change for double-digit kyu, single-digit kyu and dan players, measured both by the rating the app gives them and by the ranks they declare on the Go servers they play on.
  • 2026-09-23: The stone sound no longer stops your music. On some phones, placing a stone paused whatever was playing in another app, such as Spotify, because the app played its click the way a music player plays a track and the phone gave it the speaker. The click is now played as a short sound effect that plays over other audio instead of taking it over. On iPhone and iPad it also follows the silent switch now, so with the switch on the stones are quiet.
  • 2026-09-23: The monthly question about your ranks on other servers now opens with the answers you gave last time already filled in, on any device signed in to your account, so if nothing has changed you only need to press Submit.
  • 2026-09-22: A fix for a freeze. Pressing a button while the mouse was still moving, most easily Previous right after a correct answer, could start a browser drag of the button instead of a click, after which the window took no further input at all and only closing it helped. Nothing in the app is meant to be dragged, so dragging is now off everywhere, and dropping a file on the window no longer navigates the app away to it. Separately, the buttons now stay off until the answer overlay has actually lifted, instead of coming back while it still covered the board.
  • 2026-09-21: The leaderboard now has a past. A selector above the table switches between the usual weekly board and any month the app has kept, and a month's board lists the highest rank each player reached during it rather than where they stand now — so a good run is not forgotten when the month ends. Only players who were active in that month appear, and the month you are in stays out of the list until it is over. A past month has no Declared column, since a declared rank describes the present and no history of them is kept; a month shows a single Highest column, which follows the units selector exactly as the weekly board's Predicted column does. The weekly board is unchanged and is still what the page opens on.
  • 2026-09-21: You can give yourself a name on the leaderboard without linking a player record. Menu → Account has a new Alias row: pick one word from each of three lists — an adjective, a colour and an animal — and the three-word name stands in place of Anonymous on the board, in whichever of the app's four languages the reader is using. It is a name and nothing more: no other player can choose the same one while you hold it, it says nothing about who you are, and clearing it returns you to Anonymous and frees the words for someone else. A linked EGD or AGA record still takes precedence, as before, and unlinking brings your alias back rather than dropping you to Anonymous. The setting that keeps you anonymous still hides your alias along with everything else.
  • 2026-09-16: The board wears too. Over a sitting, hairline cracks open along the grain and stains spread on the wood, each growing a little from one problem to the next and new ones appearing as the run goes on. The marks are fixed by the sitting's first problem, so they stay where they are until a break of a minute, after which the next sitting starts on a clean board.
  • 2026-09-16: The stones are no longer one stamp. Each stone's highlight sits, stretches and shines a little differently, many have a bite out of the rim so the outline is no longer a perfect circle, a few a faint pit on the face, and some a jagged crack running in from the edge. The set is fixed by the problem, so a problem always shows the same stones with the same flaws and the next problem shows others. The stones also wear with use: a fresh sitting shows a stone or two with a defect, and the longer you solve without a break of a minute the more worn the set, until at about two hundred problems in one run nearly every stone carries one.
  • 2026-09-16: Every motif name explains itself. Tap a line in the front-page Weaknesses list, a motif name in the Weaknesses or Tendencies tab, or an entry in the review-mode "What this tests" list, and a bubble says what the motif is and what the reading behind the line means for you, with the number in it: the rank effect on problems where that move is the answer, or the share of your wrong answers that reached for it against what the board offered. A reading that only narrowly clears the bar says so.
  • 2026-09-16: A visual refresh of everything around the board, which is unchanged. Buttons, the menu, the dialogs and the statistics window now share one palette: a warm dark ground, off-white text, and a single vermilion accent kept for the things that mean something, namely the recall count, the rank progress and the active tab. The mode name is a small label instead of a heading, the rank progress bar has a knob and sits clear of the rank, and the ink-wash corners are quieter. The About, Changelog, Privacy, Methodology, Leaderboard and Statistics pages share the same palette and typography. The loading screen now stays up until the board is ready on a slow connection, instead of dropping onto a half-built page after fifteen seconds.

  • 2026-08-31: The bars on the Weaknesses and Tendencies tabs now carry a scale. Thin marks sit either side of each bar’s centre line at one and two standard errors — the same two distances the colours already step at — so how far out a reading is, in the units the app judges it by, can be seen on the bar itself rather than inferred from its shade. A mark is left off where it would fall beyond the end of the bar, and the line under each table names them. The readings themselves are unchanged.
  • 2026-08-31: Every problem has been rated afresh, by a model that is now told less about each position. Until now it was also given how strong the best moves in a position were — a fact about the answer. That is harmless for judging how hard a problem is, but the same model is what scores each individual move you might play, and there it was handing every wrong move on the board a description of the right one, which was enough to turn some readings on the Weaknesses and Tendencies tabs the wrong way round. It now works from KataGo's own read of the position and from how a range of engine settings of differing strength fare on it, and from nothing that describes the solution. Difficulty came out just as accurate — checked again against the ranks players declare on other servers, with the figures on the About page re-measured and unchanged — while the per-move readings became steadier. Separately, those readings are now weighed against a truer picture of what each problem offered. When the app works out what a typical mistake on a problem would have looked like, the available wrong moves no longer all count the same: each counts for as much as the engine actually considered it. A move it glanced at once says little about what was tempting, and a move it studied at length says a great deal, and the two were previously given equal say. Your history and your review schedule are untouched; ranks are worked out afresh from the new ratings, so yours may shift slightly as that feeds through, and the app re-reads the skill information for your recent history by itself the first time you open it.
  • 2026-08-30: The Weaknesses and Tendencies tabs now say what their bars mean, and use their colours to say how sure the app is. A line under each table states the bar as what it is: the range the app considers plausible for that reading, one standard error either side of the number — about a 68% interval, not the 95% an unlabelled bar is usually read as. That width is deliberate, because it is also the bar a reading has to clear before the app treats it as a finding, so a row takes on colour exactly when its interval clears the centre mark. The colour now comes in two steps rather than fading in: a muted shade for a reading between one and two standard errors from neutral, the full shade beyond two, and no colour at all below one. On the Tendencies tab both directions are now the same red — reaching for a skill too often and shying away from it are the same kind of finding, and the two different colours used before invited reading one of them as good news. Which way you lean is still read off the side of the centre mark the dot sits on, and the number beside the name says it exactly. Nothing about the readings themselves, your rank or your history has changed.
  • 2026-08-30: The skills the app tracks have been rechosen from the ground up, and there are now seven: life and death, yose, ko, opening, tenuki, sente and semeai. Every candidate skill was put through the same trial — can a player's standing on it actually be pinned down from a history somebody has, and does it stay the same skill when the model is retrained — and the seven that ship are the ones that passed both, several of them new: opening, sente and semeai join the set, and sente in particular is now defined more sharply, as the moves your opponent cannot afford to ignore where their answer to a mistake would have been to play elsewhere. Yose is the skill previously labelled counting — the new name says what it always measured. Territory leaves the list: the app could tell which problems turn on it, but not, with any useful speed, where any individual player stands on it, and a reading that never firms up is a promise the tab cannot keep. Retraining the whole set this way also made the surviving skills easier to read — a skill nobody's standing could be measured on was quietly diluting the evidence for its neighbours, so dropping it sharpened the rest. Ratings and ranks were refitted in the same pass and the accuracy figures on the About page re-measured. Your rank, your history and your review schedule are untouched; the app re-reads the skill information for your recent history by itself the first time you open it, and readings on the tab will shift as it does — those are the new dimensions being measured, not a change in your play.
  • 2026-08-27: The Tendencies tab no longer measures your mistakes against an even split — it measures them against your own problems. Whether a wrong move "leans toward" a skill was judged with fifty-fifty as the balance point, on the reasoning that mistakes leaning neither way are balanced. But whether a mistake can lean either way is decided by the board in front of you: the answer to a tsumego is almost never a tenuki, so nearly every wrong move on offer counts as more tenuki-like than the answer — and a player picking wrong moves at random would read as leaning toward tenuki with near certainty. Measured across the whole attempt log, that one effect — the menu of moves, not the player — accounted for essentially all of the tenuki habit the tab reported, which is why nearly every player was told they had one; on other skills it was quietly pushing readings the other way. Each of your errors is now compared against what a typical mistake on that same problem would have looked like, so the number on each row is how far your own mistakes lean beyond that — and the bar is drawn the same way, balance always at the centre, the dot's distance from it exactly the number printed. Some readings will move, and a few will change sign — those are corrections, not changes in your play. With the menu no longer mistaken for a habit, tenuki also rejoins ordinary training: it was the one skill excluded from steering your problems, precisely because nearly everyone seemed to lean on it, and that reason is gone. Your rank, your history and your review schedule are untouched; the app fills in the new per-problem information for your recent history by itself the first time you open it.
  • 2026-08-25: The privacy policy is now linked from the board itself — bottom right corner, set into the ink-wash art at the same weight as the painting, rather than being reachable only through About. It has been updated for Google sign-in in all four languages: what is stored is an anonymous identifier issued for this app alone, no address and no name, and the sign-in library is fetched from Google only if you actually open that screen.
  • 2026-08-25: You can sign in with Google. Menu → Account has a new Google row: link a Google account once, and on any other device — a new phone, a reinstall, a browser whose data you cleared — signing in with it brings your history, rank and review schedule back without you having to remember the account code. The code still is the account and still works exactly as before; Google is a way back to it, not a replacement for it, and linking one is optional. What is kept on the server is an anonymous identifier Google issues for this app alone: no email address, no name, nothing that could identify you anywhere else. One Google account belongs to one code and one code to one Google account, and Unlink — the same row — releases both. Signing in on a device that is already using a different code asks before it swaps, naming the code you would be moving to, so a sign-in can never quietly take a device off the account it was on.
  • 2026-08-25: Menu → Account now says what the Profile row is for. The first time you open it with nothing linked, a bubble points at that row: linking your European (EGD) or American (AGA) player record puts your name and official rank on the leaderboard, which is also the point of doing it — it gives everyone else a name to try to beat. It appears once, only while nothing is linked, and never again afterwards; the "?" beside the row explains the same thing whenever you want it. Separately, the tip that explains a repeat problem — that you have seen it before, and it has come back just as you were about to forget it — had never reached anyone. Each tip is checked when its moment arrives and again a moment later, just before it is drawn, and that second check had no way of knowing the problem in front of you was a repeat, so it refused every time. New players will now see it.
  • 2026-08-25: You can now put your own name on the leaderboard, without an invitation. Menu → Account has a new Leaderboard section: search the European (EGD) and American (AGA) rating databases by name or member id — one box covers both, and each result is labelled with its source, official rank and country — then pick yourself. Your name and your official rating appear on the board from then on, the rating refreshed weekly from that database as before. Nothing checks that you are the player you pick, because neither database offers any way to check: what is enforced instead is that a player can only be claimed by one account, and that you can undo it. Unlink is beside the name, and takes your name and rating off the board again; linking a different player replaces the old one. The setting that keeps you anonymous has moved here too, from the foot of the leaderboard page, so both choices about how you appear now sit in one place — it is the same setting as the one in the rank survey, and works exactly as before.   • 2026-08-24: The Weaknesses tab could sit empty, saying there was not enough data yet, on a device with a long history behind it — and while it did, the app also stopped aiming problems at your weak points, and the panel shown while solving named none. One stored attempt was enough to cause it. Every attempt keeps a record of what the position and your move called on, written in the terms of the skill dimensions in use the day you answered it; when those dimensions are relearned, the app drops those records and re-reads them, so they all speak the same language again. Two things could slip past that. The app updates itself between problems, but it only finds out an update exists when it reports an answer — so an app open from before an update always got to record one more attempt in the old terms first. And the problem waiting for you, and the one on your screen when the update landed, were each stored whole, with the terms they were chosen under, and were not part of what got dropped. The app now refuses to write those terms at all when it can see they are out of date, drops every place one can be kept rather than only your attempts, and re-reads anything still missing on any load rather than waiting for the next relearning. Attempts already stored in the old terms are checked once against the published data and corrected. Nothing about your rank, your history or your reviews was affected, and the two readings themselves are unchanged.
  • 2026-08-23: The Weaknesses tab now shows every skill, always, each with two readings and a bar showing how sure the app is — in place of the short list of findings that happened to clear a bar. The first reading is whether a skill costs you accuracy beyond what the problem's difficulty already accounts for, given in ranks. The second is which way your mistakes lean, given as the share of them that would have to go the other way to be evenly balanced: if six of your ten mistakes on a skill reached for it and four shied away, one of the ten would have to change, and it reads +10%. Both are now worked out from your own attempts and nothing else. Until now each was measured against an average taken over all players — and since harder problems turn on different skills (about one problem in twenty-five at beginner level hinges on tenuki, against nearly one in three higher up), that average is largely a statement about the level you play at, so part of what you were being told about your play was your rank wearing a disguise. One skill was affected badly enough to be plainly wrong: through a flaw in how that average was applied, tenuki was reported as a fault to very nearly every player, whatever they actually did. Both numbers now stand against a fixed point of your own — no effect on your accuracy, and mistakes that lean neither way — so no one else's play enters the reckoning at all. The same two readings now also choose which problems you are served, so what the tab tells you and what you are handed to practise can no longer disagree. Tenuki is the single exception: almost every player really does lean towards it, so it is still reported to you but no longer steers your problems, since a fault shared by nearly everyone would push every player into the same corner of the collection.
  • 2026-08-23: Over-reliance on a skill is now trained against, not just reported. When the evidence says you reach for a skill too often — playing tenuki when the position wanted something else, say — the app now serves you problems whose answer is not that skill: the situations where the temptation exists and is wrong, which is exactly where an over-used instinct needs the practice. It cannot see which wrong moves a problem offers, but it does not need to: where the answer is not tenuki, tenuki is on average among the tempting mistakes, so aiming there hits the right problems in expectation. Such a case appears in your weaknesses list as a skill you overvalue, to keep it apart from an ordinary weakness. And before claiming it, the app checks the evidence is not simply that you are good at the skill — being strong at tenuki also leaves a similar statistical trace, and that earns a strength report, not a training campaign.
  • 2026-08-23: Your current weaknesses are now shown while you solve, and the Weaknesses tab can report strengths. While solving, the small panel that in review mode explains what a problem tests now lists — under the title "Weaknesses" — the skills your problems are currently being aimed at, strongest first. It shows the standing list only, never which skill today's problem was picked for, so it gives nothing away about the position in front of you; and when nothing is flagged it simply stays out of sight. The first time the list ever appears, a one-off bubble points it out. In the Weaknesses tab, evidence that you are unusually good at a named skill is no longer discarded: it is reported as a strength, with the same standards of evidence and the same wording tiers as a weakness — merely suspected until the evidence is firm. And the bar a skill must clear — to be listed as a problem's motif, to become a training focus, and to appear as a finding — is now one and the same everywhere, so the three can never quietly drift apart.
  • 2026-08-23: Problems aimed at your weak points are back, chosen one weak point at a time. Once the app is confident of your rank, each skill it has reason to believe is troubling you gets a turn: a new problem is drawn for one of them at random, and it is the problem, among the fifty nearest your level, that features that skill most strongly. What it will not do is serve any single weak point more than half the time, and ordinary problems — chosen for nothing but sitting at your level — always keep a turn of their own. That restraint is not caution about annoying you — it is what keeps the diagnosis honest. If everything you saw were, say, ko problems, the app could no longer tell whether you struggle with ko or are simply having a rough patch overall: the two look identical in an unbroken run of one kind, and the misread would quietly lower your rank instead of sharpening the weakness. Mixing in ordinary problems is exactly what keeps those two stories distinguishable, so ordinary problems take a turn alongside your weak points however many of those there are: with one weak point, half and half; with two, a third each. The Weaknesses tab now also speaks with two levels of conviction: a finding the evidence merely leans towards is introduced with "We suspect:", and only one the evidence has pinned down firmly is stated outright. Which problems are chosen for a suspicion and for a firm finding is the same; only the wording differs. While the app is still working out your rank, nothing changes: it keeps picking the problems that tell it the most, as before.
  • 2026-08-21: The black "Click to continue" screen is gone. It was there for a rare mishap — a phone short of memory can throw away the drawn board while the app is in the background — and tapping it asked for the board to be drawn afresh. Last week's attempt to keep it out of the way was to raise it a moment after the app was sent away rather than instantly; that did not help, because a backgrounded app is usually frozen outright, so the wait was never served while you were away and ran the moment you came back instead, painting the black screen straight into your return. Either way the screen was already standing there whenever the app came back, and taking it down again was a race against the first thing your phone drew — one the app lost on most switches. The app now watches for the drawing itself. A backgrounded app is not drawn at all, so the first thing drawn is proof that you are back, whether or not the phone gets round to telling the app so — and a return the app is never told about is the one case the black screen existed for. The board is rebuilt there and then, leaving nothing to tap and nothing in your way. Nothing about your rank or your history is involved.
  • 2026-08-19: The 13×13 collection has grown by about 20,600 problems, from roughly 39,000 to roughly 68,000 — it is now well over half the size of the 9×9 one, where before it was a third. Every difficulty in the whole collection has been refitted to take them in, so your rank may shift slightly as that feeds through, and the app re-derives your stored history the first time you open it. The Weaknesses tab's skill dimensions have been relearned on the enlarged collection as well; they are the same six as before — life and death against territory, counting against shape, ko against opening — so nothing changes about what that tab can tell you. The translations of your rank into other servers' scales have been refitted at the same time. Nothing has been withdrawn and no attempt of yours has been touched. One note if you compare the About page with what it said before: the 9×9 figure there has come down slightly, from about 118,000 to about 113,000, but no problems were removed now — that number had simply been left stale since earlier withdrawals, and counting it afresh brought it into line.
  • 2026-08-19: Opening a shared problem link could leave your next problem far too easy — a 3 dan could be handed a 2 kyu problem, and it was still waiting the following day. Your device does not hold the whole collection: it downloads it in bands of similar difficulty, keeping the ones around your own level. Opening someone else's link fetches the band that problem belongs to and nothing else, so for the rest of that visit the only problems on your device were the ones near the difficulty of the problem you were sent. The app picks your next problem as soon as you answer the one you are on — and during such a visit it had nothing to pick from but that band, so it took the closest match it could find there, which could be a long way from your level. That choice is remembered for next time, which is why it survived until the following day. The app now chooses only from an unbroken stretch of downloaded bands that takes in your own, so whatever it picks is genuinely near your level even when little has been downloaded; and if your own band is not on the device, it fetches that first rather than settling for what happens to be at hand. It keeps the bands just outside the ones it is choosing from ready in advance, so none of this costs you any waiting as your rank moves. When your band truly cannot be fetched — with no connection, say — it falls back to the nearest bands you do hold and tells you it is serving away from your level, exactly as before. Nothing about your rank or your history was affected, and shared links themselves are unchanged: they are still view-only and still never counted.
  • 2026-08-14: Leaving the app and coming back could put a black screen reading "Click to continue" in your way — on Android often on every single switch. That screen is there for a rare mishap: a phone short of memory can throw away the drawn board while the app is in the background, and tapping the screen asks for it to be drawn afresh. It was being raised the instant the app was sent away, but a phone carries on drawing an app for a moment after that, while it slides off the screen — so the black screen was caught in the picture the task switcher shows you, and was still standing there when you came back. It then outstayed its welcome, because the app rebuilt the board before allowing the screen to be taken down, and the black screen was the last thing drawn until that finished. It is now raised only once the app has stopped being drawn, so an ordinary switch never catches it, and on your return it comes down first and the board is rebuilt after. It still appears, and can still be tapped, on a return the app is never told about — which is the case it exists for.
  • 2026-08-13: For a few players the wooden board texture had vanished, leaving a flat tan board behind — and once it happened it stayed that way, through reloads and through every update. The app keeps its own copy of the handful of files it always needs, so that they load instantly and still work with no connection. Through a fault in how those copies were stored a second copy of the board texture could be left behind, and from the first update onwards the app read that one instead of the fresh one; if it had been saved badly there was nothing to put it right, because a background picture is only ever asked for once. Those files are now always read from the single place that is rebuilt on every update, any stray duplicate is cleared out, and a hiccup while saving one of them no longer costs the whole set. Affected boards come back on the next update. Nothing about the problems, your history or your rank was involved.
  • 2026-08-13: Review reminders no longer make a sound, and they now count down as you work through them. A reminder has nothing urgent attached to it — the problems are due, not about to vanish — so it arrives quietly and waits in the notification tray rather than announcing itself. Once it is there it keeps up with you: each review you answer drops the number it shows, and clearing the last one takes the reminder away by itself. Until now it was meant to disappear as soon as you touched the board, on the reasoning that by then it had done its job — but on Android it usually did not disappear at all. A reminder delivered while the app is closed can no longer be reached by the app once you reopen it, so it sat in the tray indefinitely, still claiming problems were waiting long after they had been reviewed. Nothing changes about which problems are due, or when a reminder is sent.
  • 2026-08-10: Once the app is confident of your rank it starts choosing problems by the skills that have been tripping you up — and that choice was barely working. Two things were wrong with it. First, it judged how well a problem matched your weak points on an absolute scale, and almost all of what it was reading turned out to be identical for every player: a standing preference for certain kinds of position, coming from the way wrong answers tend to differ from right ones across the whole collection rather than from anything about you. Everyone was being steered towards the same corner of the collection and told it was personal. What every player's reading has in common is now measured and taken out, so what remains is only how you differ from everybody else. Second, having worked out which candidates suited you best, the app then picked among them close to at random — the differences between problems at your level are small, and the way it weighed them left the choice almost indistinguishable from ignoring the weakness reading altogether. It now simply takes the best candidate, both while it is still working out your rank (where it takes the problem that tells it the most) and afterwards (where it takes the one that best fits your weak points). Together these make the weakness-driven choice about eight times as strong as before, and it now points somewhere genuinely different for different players. You will not see less variety: a problem you have answered is not offered again for a long time, so the best remaining candidate is a different problem each time.
  • 2026-08-09: The About page now says what the app's accuracy is worth in games. "Within one rank of what a player declares" is hard to picture, so the same comparison is also stated as a tournament record: how many games an ordinary rating would need in order to be this accurate. Based on published statistics from the European Go Database, and measured on the players whose European rating is machine-verified rather than self-reported, the rank shown here agrees with a player's real rank about as closely as two independent ratings built from about sixteen tournament games agree with each other, give or take three. Nothing about your rank changes; this is a new way of describing how good it already is.
  • 2026-08-09: The whole collection has been re-checked, with a stricter test than the one used a week ago — and that test says the earlier one was wrong in both directions. 1,374 problems withdrawn on the 2nd and the 8th are back: they were sound all along, and the checks that removed them had been reading lines the computer started exploring and then abandoned, treating an unfinished line as a verdict. In the other direction, 4,032 problems have now been withdrawn that those checks missed. The new test only looks at lines played all the way to the end — both players passing, the game genuinely over — and asks two things of each problem: that the move you are asked to find still wins when the opponent tries everything recorded against it, and that every other first move really does lose. Anything short of the end is ignored rather than guessed at, which is exactly what went wrong before. Your attempts on withdrawn problems are kept this time. Last week's withdrawal took your history with it; this one does not, so your totals and solve counts stay as they were, and only problems you have not yet seen are affected. Difficulties are being refitted on the corrected collection, so your rank may shift slightly as that feeds through.
  • 2026-08-09: Stepping back through an answer now follows the lines that were actually played out, rather than working out which to show each time you open one. Each problem file now says outright which continuations were read to a finish, and the app follows those. Before, it looked for any pass anywhere below a move and took that as a sign the line had been seen through to the end — which is not the same thing, and is how a continuation that had never been properly explored could be offered to you and then drift into a lost position. The opponent's replies you are shown are chosen exactly as before, so you will still sometimes see two of them; they are simply now drawn from lines that genuinely finish.
